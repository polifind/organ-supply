Organ Failure Is a Supply Problem

Pig organs already work in people. The only problem left is making them last.

Everyone assumes your organs wear out and then you die. Much of modern medicine is built around that assumption: whole specialties exist to slow the failure of organs nobody can replace. But it is an assumption, not a law of nature.

A kidney, a heart, a liver or the lungs stop working, and there is not much anyone can do. A transplant needs a stranger to die at the right time, in the right place, and to match you. Most people never get one. There was no replacement part.

More than 37 million Americans have chronic kidney disease, and nearly 750,000 have irreversible kidney failure. The waiting list holds 95,000 of them. Nine people a day come off it because they died; thirteen more because they had grown too sick to transplant.

The reason is never that medicine failed. It is that there was no part.

An organ is a part. Parts can be replaced. The supply has just never existed. A world where it does, and where a failing organ is something you swap rather than something you die of, needs a name. We call it Organ Abundance.

What changed

In 2024 that changed, with almost no notice outside the field. Surgeons at Massachusetts General Hospital transplanted gene-edited pig kidneys into living people, and the kidneys worked: filtering, making urine, keeping a patient off dialysis. Human organs are scarce. A pig is not. You can make more.

Pig organs are a manufactured product, and that one fact reorganizes everything downstream. The donor is no longer a tragedy to be waited for. The organ can be characterized before implantation, scheduled instead of raced against the clock, and refined between versions. None of that is possible when the source is a dying stranger.

The durability problem

Today's pig organs do not last. The longest any has worked in a living person is 271 days, about nine months, before the graft failed. That patient returned to dialysis and later received a human kidney, the first person ever to cross from a pig organ to a human one. The bridge holds. It is just far too short.

This is engineering, not discovery. A pig organ can keep a person alive. What nobody knows yet is which modifications make it last.

A pig kidney is not uniformly foreign. It is close to human in the machinery that makes an organ work, and conspicuously foreign in the surface proteins the immune system inspects. Finding those proteins was the easy part. Removing them is harder, because many of them do a job the organ needs.

Whatever stays foreign has to be suppressed with drugs, and the drugs cause their own harm. Every antigen removed is a drug the patient does not have to take.

Those modifications can be found. The plausible combinations are enormous in number; the ones worth trying are not. The limit was never anyone's understanding of rejection. It was speed. Testing one combination has meant building a whole animal and waiting years for a single answer, and no one can search a large space at that rate. That is what has changed. Most of the search can now happen before an animal is built.

Why this is a longevity question

Look at what kills people in a rich country and you find the same short list of organs every time. The heart stops pumping, the lungs stop exchanging, the liver stops clearing, the kidneys stop filtering. Cancer and dementia aside, most people do not die of anything exotic. They die because one organ reaches the end of its service life and there is no replacement part. That is the fact this whole field is aimed at.

Medicine has spent a century slowing each of those failures and has never once reversed one. Statins slow the heart's decline and antifibrotics the lung's; dialysis stands in for the kidney without ever restoring it. Each manages a descent. None turns the curve back upward.

Replacement is the only longevity intervention already proven in humans. It is called transplantation.

Put a working organ into a person whose own has failed and they gain years they would not otherwise have had. That was settled decades ago. Its only limit has been that each organ requires a stranger to die.

The other route is repair: drugs that clear out worn-out cells, treatments that wind a cell's internal clock backwards. Some of it is working. Partial reprogramming has reversed vision loss in aged mice and eased the hallmarks of aging in vivo; senescent-cell clearing drugs have reached human trials, so far for safety rather than effect.

But repair is at an earlier stage against a harder target, because an aged organ has thousands of things wrong with it at once and a failed organ needs only to be swapped. If repair works it makes replacement unnecessary, and until then the two are complements rather than rivals.

Nobody expects a car to die because its alternator fails. You replace the alternator. The car outlives every part it was built with, and this strikes no one as remarkable. Bodies are not machines, and the analogy breaks in the places biology always breaks analogies. But on this one point it holds exactly.

A system whose parts can be replaced has a life expectancy set by something other than its parts.

A failing kidney and an old kidney are the same problem, and a new one fixes both. So the goal is not curing kidney disease. It is building the supply of spare parts that a long life requires, starting with the part that has the largest market and the most forgiving failure mode.

Kidneys come first because dialysis lets the patient wait and a failed graft is survivable. There is no dialysis for a heart, a liver or a lung. For those organs the waiting list is the disease and a failed graft is usually fatal, which makes them harder targets and larger prizes.

Solve supply once and you have solved it for the whole set, because the immunology, manufacturing, regulatory path and surgical capacity are largely shared. A pig kidney that lasts ten years proves out the platform that replaces the other three.

Every route to the same place

Gene-edited pigs are not the only road. Several groups are making genuinely different bets, and which one gets through is an open question.

Pigs raise no new ethical question. More than a billion pigs are raised and slaughtered for food every year, and a society comfortable with that has already settled the argument about whether a pig's life may be used for a human's benefit. Using a few thousand of them to end an organ shortage is a far smaller claim than the one made every day at lunch. The conditions are better too: a designated-pathogen-free facility is cleaner and more closely monitored than any commercial farm.

Xenotransplantation, editing animal organs, the only approach that has kept a human being alive on a manufactured organ, with durability its one remaining problem.

Growing human organs inside animals. Engineer an animal embryo so it cannot form a given organ, add the patient's own stem cells, and let them fill the gap. That would dissolve rejection entirely, but the work is still confined to embryos.

Growing tissue from stem cells. Excellent for studying disease in a dish, but such tissue has no filtering structures, no drainage and no blood supply of its own, and nothing you cannot pump blood through can be implanted.

Stripping a donor organ to its scaffold and reseeding it with the recipient's own cells, so the structure is natural and the cells are not foreign, demonstrated in animals but recovering only partial function.

Printing the organ. The most general solution and the furthest away, because printing a network of capillaries at the fineness a kidney needs is not a scaling problem but an unsolved one.

Why now: biology and AI

You cannot prompt an organ into existence. The information is not in the literature. It lives only in the organ, and only if someone runs the experiment.

Observational data describes states already seen; it cannot predict interventions never tried. No model can deduce what a given edit does to a given organ until someone does the experiment, which is why the value accrues to whoever generates the data rather than whoever reads it.

But the design half of the loop is moving fast. In August 2026 two mathematicians working with language models proved that smooth fluid flows can blow up in finite time, a question open for decades. The proofs were formally verified in Lean within a week.

Biology is following, a few years behind and on a harder problem, because a proof can be checked in software and a cell cannot. AlphaFold 3 now predicts how proteins bind DNA, RNA and small molecules. Virtual cells aim to simulate a perturbation before anyone runs it. And a system published in Nature in 2026 proposed drug candidates that were later confirmed in the laboratory.

Xeno is unusually well suited to this. The question is which pig surfaces a human immune system attacks and what happens when you change them: protein structure, binding and immune recognition, which is what these models have got good at.

A model can rank thousands of candidate edits overnight. Only the laboratory can say which ranking was right, so speed comes from shortening the distance between those two steps. It also comes from measurement good enough that a failure names its own mechanism rather than a date on a survival curve. Traces of the organ's own DNA shed into the blood already flag injury in a study of nearly three thousand kidney recipients before standard blood tests do.

What the economics look like

Organ supply in America is already nationalized, though it is rarely described that way. The National Organ Transplant Act of 1984 made it a federal crime to transfer a human organ for valuable consideration and created a single federally contracted network to decide who gets what. When supply cannot grow, the only question left is who gets the one kidney, and a country answers that with law.

Every rule in transplantation is a rationing rule. You ration what you cannot manufacture.

Manufacturing dissolves the premise. The list, the priority points, the whole ethics of who deserves the organ exist because the input is a dying stranger.

The buyer already exists. Since 1972 Medicare has covered kidney failure regardless of age, which makes the federal government the largest payer for organ replacement in the country. It is buying the expensive version.

Medicare spends more than $130 billion, over 24 percent of total spending, on patients with kidney disease, and the National Kidney Foundation warns that untreated progression to kidney failure could bankrupt the Medicare Trust Fund. Every dollar of it buys maintenance, not repair. Two companies run most of the world's dialysis on roughly $34 billion of combined annual revenue, much of it from government payers.

Thirty-four billion dollars a year, worldwide, and not one organ fixed.

The market prices those businesses at about $48 billion combined, counting debt, which is under 1.5 times a single year of revenue. That is dialysis valued as an annuity on a population that cannot get better, not as a growth industry.

A cure is precisely what a durable organ is. The incumbent valuation is not this category's ceiling. It is the floor being replaced.

The surgery itself ranks among the most valuable in medicine. Billed charges for a double-lung transplant reach $2.3 million, a heart $1.9 million, and a kidney $446,800. The kidney is the cheapest of them, and still close to half a million dollars a time across roughly 28,000 operations a year.

Today the binding input is free and unobtainable, a dead stranger who matches. Make that input manufacturable and the scarcest step in the most expensive procedures in medicine becomes a product with a supplier.

Whoever produces organs at scale is not selling a therapy for one disease. They are selling the part, for most of the ways people die. Price a durable replacement below the lifetime cost of managing the failure and the arithmetic works in every organ market at once.

That is a trillion-dollar position, and it will not stay entirely private. Every developed government has decided organ allocation is too consequential to leave to markets, and manufacturing does nothing to reverse that. Expect a regulated utility at extraordinary scale rather than an unconstrained monopoly. It would still be one of the most valuable things anyone could build.

What needs to happen now

Surgeons and the people who train them. Open more training positions this year, because the pipeline is longer than the biology.

Transplant physicians and immunologists. Immunosuppression is the shared bottleneck of every route to a manufactured organ, and a regimen that is durable without being toxic is worth as much as any organ.

Biologists, engineers, AI researchers and founders. Tolerance induction, regulatory-cell therapy, automated cell-line engineering, perfusion hardware and immune-monitoring assays are each worth more in a world with unlimited organs.

Regulators worldwide. Decide now what the approval pathway is for a manufactured organ, and what the evidentiary standard for durability is when the endpoint is ten years and the trial cannot be.

Industrial operators. Build the designated-pathogen-free facilities, cold chain and quality systems, because this is a physical supply chain like any other.

Payers and health systems. Model what happens to costs when a durable organ becomes purchasable, and do it before the first invoice.

Two other curves are bending underneath all of it: AI that designs and predicts, and robotics that will eventually relieve the shortage of surgeons and operating rooms that unlimited organs would expose. Neither substitutes for the biology. Both change how quickly it can be searched.

None of this requires anyone to agree about pigs. It only requires agreeing that Organ Abundance is coming, and that the parts outside the laboratory have the longer lead times.

The leap

Most predictions ask you to believe something that has not happened yet. This one rests on something that already has. A pig organ has kept a person alive. What remains is making it last.

You do not have to believe a cell can be made young, or anything about aging at all. You need only believe that a part which works for months can be made to work for years. That is still hard. It means finding modifications nobody has found yet, and proving they hold for a decade. The best durability anyone has achieved is about two years in a monkey and under a year in a human, against a target of ten.

A hard leap, but not an impossible one.

The world still treats organ failure as permanent. It is not. Medicine has always organized itself around scarcity: triage, allocation, waiting lists, the whole moral architecture of deciding who gets the one kidney. Once replacement becomes easier than repair, all of that reorganizes around abundance.

Replace parts as they wear out, and organ failure stops being a way you die.
