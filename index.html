Organ Failure Is a Supply Problem

Everyone assumes your organs wear out and then you die. We have built much of modern medicine around that assumption: whole specialties exist to slow the failure of organs we cannot replace. But it is an assumption, not a law of nature. Organ failure is a supply problem, and the supply can now be built.

A kidney, a heart, a liver, or the lungs stop working, and there is not much anyone can do. If you are lucky, you get a transplant, but a transplant needs a stranger to die at the right time and match you. Most people never get one. We are surgeons, and we watch them die on the waitlist.

An organ is a part. Parts can be replaced. We have just never had the supply.

What Changed

Last year that changed, and almost no one noticed. Surgeons at Massachusetts General Hospital put gene-edited pig kidneys into living people, and for the first time a manufactured organ kept a person alive. A human organ is scarce because someone has to die and match. A pig is not. You can make more.

But today's pig organs fail too soon. The record is under a year. They were designed to survive the immediate rejection after surgery, not the slower kind that follows. What is left is not whether it works, but which edits make it durable.

The organ shortage is now an engineering and industrial problem, not a medical one.

The Search

The number of plausible combinations is enormous, but the number worth trying is not. The limit was not our understanding of rejection. It was speed.

Testing one combination meant building a whole pig and waiting years for a single answer. No one could search a space that expensive.

That is what has now changed. New tools can predict which edits are likely to matter, test many at once, and learn from each result to guide the next. The field can finally learn faster than it builds pigs. The organs already function for months. A pig organ that lasts for years can be built this decade.

Replacement

Once organs are something you can make, you stop waiting for one to fail before you replace it. You replace it early, the way people already replace a hip at sixty or get Lasik instead of living in glasses.

There is no reason to stop at sick organs. A failing kidney and an old kidney are the same problem, and a new one fixes both.

Replace parts as they wear out, and organ failure stops being a way you die.

Most predictions ask you to believe something that has not happened yet. This one rests on something that already has. A pig organ has kept a person alive.

You do not have to believe anything about aging, or any biology we do not yet understand. You only have to believe that a part that works for months can be made to work for years. That is a small leap, not a large one.

What It Costs

It is not free. Long-term rejection may need more edits, or trickier ones, than expected. Making genetically identical animals at scale is its own hard problem. Getting the organ approved is slow, and the process can turn political. That is why this takes a decade and not a year. None of it makes the problem unsolvable. It only means someone has to solve it.

We are surgeons, engineers, and founders. This is the problem we have set out to solve.

The world still treats organ failure as permanent. It is not. The first customers will be decabillionaires buying their own organ supply. And then every government in the world.

Medicine has spent its whole history repairing organs because it could not replace them. Once organs can be manufactured, medicine reorganizes around replacement.
