Organ Failure Is a Supply Problem

Pig organs already work in people. Now we have to make them last.

Everyone assumes your organs wear out and then you die. Much of modern medicine is built around that assumption: whole specialties exist to slow the failure of organs nobody can replace.

But it is only an assumption.

When a kidney, heart, liver, or lung stops working, there is no good way to replace it. The only real option is a transplant from a human donor, which requires an organ to become available at the right time, in the right place, and to be suitable for the person who needs it. There have never been enough.

More than 35 million American adults have chronic kidney disease. More than 800,000 are living with kidney failure, on dialysis or with a transplant. About 95,000 are currently waiting for a kidney.

For many of them, the limiting technology is not another drug. It is another kidney.

An organ is a part, and parts can be replaced. The supply has just never existed.

A world where it does, where a failing organ becomes something you replace instead of something you die of, deserves a name.

We call it Organ Abundance.

What changed

In 2024, surgeons at Massachusetts General Hospital transplanted gene-edited pig kidneys into living people. The kidneys worked: filtering blood, making urine, and keeping patients off dialysis.

For most of the history of transplantation, the problem was where the next organ would come from. Human organs are donated. A pig organ can be made, grown in an animal whose genes have been edited so the organ can work in a human body.

And because it can be made, it can be designed.

The donor is no longer a tragedy you wait for. The organ can be characterized before it goes in, surgery can be scheduled instead of raced against a clock, and one generation of organs can be compared with the next. A failure can lead directly to a change in the next design.

And design problems are where the new AI-driven computational tools are strongest. Which surfaces to change on a pig organ is a question about protein structure and immune recognition, an unusually good match for what these models now predict well.

For forty years, xenotransplantation, transplanting organs from animals into people, was largely a preclinical field. It is becoming a clinical one. Living patients now have gene-edited pig kidneys, and formal clinical trials are beginning.

We have crossed an important line: a pig organ can sustain human life. Now we have to make it do so reliably, safely, and for years rather than months.

The durability problem

The best pig kidneys in living people are now working for months, not minutes or days.

The longest in a living person ran 271 days. That patient returned to dialysis and later received a human kidney, the first person ever to cross from a pig organ to a human one.

That is remarkable progress. It is also nowhere near the goal. A kidney should last years. Ideally, decades.

The first major immune barrier was relatively obvious. Humans carry natural antibodies against sugars on the surface of pig cells. Put an unmodified pig organ into a human and those antibodies can destroy it almost immediately.

Gene editing changed that. The genes responsible for three major carbohydrate targets can now be removed. Human genes can also be added to help the organ regulate complement, coagulation, inflammation, and other incompatibilities between the two species.

Those changes got the field across a threshold that once looked impossible.

What remains is harder.

The pig genome is close to ours in size and gene content, which is why a pig organ fits and functions in a human body at all. But a pig organ still presents thousands of molecular differences to a human immune system. Some matter. Many probably do not. Some can be edited safely; others perform functions the organ needs.

And the organ still has to coexist with human antibodies, immune cells, complement, clotting, and inflammatory systems for years.

Whatever incompatibility cannot be engineered away has to be controlled in the recipient. That usually means immunosuppressive drugs, which create problems of their own.

We do not yet know the combination of gene edits and immune control that produces durable function. And the number of possible combinations is enormous.

Until recently, testing a new organ design ultimately meant making the pig, raising it, transplanting the organ, and waiting to see what happened. Each answer could take months or years.

No one can search a space this large one animal at a time.

Why now

Two things are happening at once. Xenotransplantation has finally crossed into humans, just as AI is beginning to make biology computable.

Gene editing can now remove some of the molecular differences that cause the human immune system to attack a pig organ, and add human genes that help protect it. New immunosuppressive strategies can target immune pathways that conventional transplant drugs do not adequately control.

Perfusion technology can keep whole organs alive outside the body. Molecular tools can measure the response at a resolution that did not exist a decade ago.

At the same time, AI has made a different kind of progress possible. Biology is the next frontier for AI, and it has become central to the strategy of the largest AI labs.

AI can now predict the structures of proteins with extraordinary accuracy, increasingly predict how molecules interact, and design proteins that have never existed in nature. These models are still far from simulating a human immune system, but that is the direction of travel.

The ultimate version of this is straightforward to imagine: model enough of human biology that you can design a pig organ on a computer, predict how a human immune system will respond to it, change the design, and run the experiment again before making a single cell.

We are not there yet. Today, the opportunity is to build the experimental loop that gets us closer.

Models can rank candidate edits before they are made. Edited pig cells can be exposed to human serum and immune cells. Promising designs can move into whole organs kept alive on perfusion machines. Molecular measurements can show which pathways are being activated and where a design is failing.

Those results become data for the next prediction. Only the best designs need to move into animals.

A computer can narrow the search today. As the models improve, more of the search can move onto the computer. That matters because the design space is enormous.

If ten thousand possibilities can become fifty experiments, and fifty experiments can become three animals, development gets faster. If eventually ten thousand biological experiments can become ten thousand simulations, it changes again entirely.

AI and experiment are not competing approaches. Each makes the other better. Every prediction still has to meet human blood, and for now, ultimately a living body. But every experiment should also make the next prediction better.

Measurement closes that loop. For most of transplant history, a failing organ gave relatively crude signals: its function worsened, a biopsy showed injury, or eventually the organ failed. Often the answer came late.

Now we can watch the response at much higher resolution: proteins and antibodies in the blood, gene expression in tissue, individual immune-cell populations, and donor-derived cell-free DNA, the organ's own DNA shed into the bloodstream, as an early signal of injury.

A failure that shows exactly what broke points directly toward the next design. It is worth more than a success no one can explain.

The long-term goal is not simply to make animal experiments faster. It is to need fewer of them because we can predict more before they happen. That is what AI changes.

Why this is a longevity question

Medicine usually attacks organ failure upstream. Control blood pressure. Treat diabetes. Lower cholesterol. Suppress autoimmunity. Slow fibrosis. Keep an organ functioning for as long as possible.

That work matters enormously. But once enough tissue has been lost, slowing the process is no longer the same as restoring what is gone.

Replacement attacks the problem from the other end. We already know the principle works. That is what transplantation is.

Give a functioning kidney to someone whose own kidneys have failed and, for an appropriate candidate, they can live longer and better than they would on dialysis. The problem is that there are nowhere near enough organs.

That is why organ replacement belongs in the conversation about longevity. Aging is not one disease, and replacing an organ will not make someone young. But many lives still end because an essential organ can no longer do its job. If that organ becomes replaceable, that particular limit changes.

Think of a car. No one expects it to reach the end of its life because the alternator fails. You replace the alternator. A body is not a car. But the analogy is useful in one respect: a system behaves differently when its critical components become replaceable.

The goal is not immortality or the end of aging. It is to make organ failure reversible.

Why kidneys come first

Kidneys are the natural place to begin. A patient can survive without functioning kidneys because dialysis can temporarily do part of their job. If a kidney transplant fails, the patient can usually return to dialysis. There is no equivalent safety net for a heart, liver, or lung.

That makes the kidney unusually forgiving as the first manufactured solid organ. It also makes the need enormous.

A durable pig kidney would not automatically solve the heart, liver, and lung. Each organ has its own physiology and its own problems. But much of the platform is shared: source-animal engineering, immune control, infection surveillance, manufacturing, monitoring, regulation, and the infrastructure required to deliver an organ safely to a patient.

Solve those problems once and the next organ does not start from zero.

Other roads to the same place

Pigs are not the only possible source of replacement organs. Researchers are trying to grow human organs inside animals, build them from stem cells, repopulate donor scaffolds with new cells, and eventually manufacture organs from the ground up.

Any of these approaches could work. Several probably will.

But xenotransplantation has something none of the others yet does: it has put manufactured solid organs into living people and kept them alive. That is why we start there.

The use of pigs also raises a legitimate animal-welfare question. But society already raises and kills pigs at enormous scale for food. If we can make these organs safe and durable, it is difficult to imagine a more consequential use for them.

What the economics look like

The buyer already exists. Since 1972 Medicare has covered kidney failure regardless of age, which makes the federal government the largest payer for organ replacement in the country. It is buying the expensive version.

Medicare spends more than $130 billion, over 24 percent of total spending, on patients with kidney disease, and the National Kidney Foundation warns that untreated progression to kidney failure could bankrupt the Medicare Trust Fund. Every dollar of it buys maintenance, not repair.

Two companies run most of the world's dialysis on roughly $34 billion of combined annual revenue, much of it from government payers.

A durable kidney is valuable not because an organ should command an enormous price, but because the alternative already does.

The surgery itself ranks among the most valuable in medicine. Billed charges for a double-lung transplant reach $2.3 million, a heart $1.9 million, and a kidney $446,800. The kidney is the cheapest of them, and still close to half a million dollars a time across roughly 28,000 operations a year.

A kidney alone could carry a price of $500,000 to $1,000,000 in the United States. That price comes from what the alternative costs. Dialysis runs roughly $90,000 per patient per year, every year, for as long as someone survives on it. A decade of that approaches a million dollars, and a durable organ replaces the entire stream.

That price justifies building the whole enterprise now: the R&D, the pathogen-free facilities, the supply chain. Pig organs already work in people, so the core science is derisked, and the economic case no longer waits on a breakthrough.

Whoever produces organs at scale is not selling a therapy for one disease. They are selling the part, for most of the ways people die. Price a durable replacement below the lifetime cost of managing the failure and the math works in every organ market at once.

That is a trillion-dollar position, and it will not stay entirely private. Every developed government has decided organ allocation is too consequential to leave to markets, and manufacturing does nothing to reverse that. Expect a regulated utility at extraordinary scale rather than an unconstrained monopoly. It would still be one of the most valuable things anyone could build.

Organ supply in America is already nationalized. The National Organ Transplant Act of 1984 made it a federal crime to transfer a human organ for valuable consideration and created a single federally contracted network to decide who gets what.

When supply cannot grow, the only question left is who gets the one kidney, and a country answers that with law. Manufacturing dissolves the premise. The question stops being who gets the kidney? and becomes how do we make enough, and how do we make sure everyone who needs one can get one?

What needs to happen now

Most of what this future needs takes longer to build than people realize. Some of it should start before the biology is solved.

Make the immune response computable. The loop described above is only as good as the data it learns from, and most of that data does not exist yet. The field needs large, standardized datasets of how human antibodies, complement, and immune cells respond to edited pig cells and perfused organs, linked to what happened in the animal or patient. Train models on that until each new organ design can start on a computer rather than in an animal. Whoever builds that dataset holds the fastest path to a durable organ.

Surgeons, and the people who train them. Open more training positions now, because the pipeline is longer than the biology.

Roboticists and AI researchers, to automate the operation. A manufactured organ turns transplantation from a midnight emergency into a scheduled, repeatable procedure, exactly the kind of task machines learn best. An AI-driven robot has already performed the key steps of a gallbladder removal on pig tissue on its own, after learning from surgical video. Train such systems on thousands of standardized implantations, and the number of patients who can be treated stops being limited by the number of transplant surgeons.

Transplant physicians and immunologists. Immune control is the shared bottleneck of every route to a manufactured organ, and a regimen that is durable without being toxic is worth as much as any organ.

Biologists, engineers, and founders. Tolerance induction, regulatory-cell therapy, automated cell-line engineering, perfusion hardware, and immune-monitoring assays each become more valuable in a world where organs are no longer scarce.

Regulators worldwide. Decide now what the approval pathway is for a manufactured organ, and what counts as evidence of durability when the target is ten years and the trial cannot be.

Industrial operators. Build the designated-pathogen-free facilities, cold chain, and quality systems. The end state looks less like a hospital pharmacy and more like a fulfillment network: organs made at a few central sites, held in inventory, and dispatched on demand to wherever the patient is.

Design allocation from scratch. Today's system is built to ration a scarce, perishable gift. Abundance calls for models that predict which organ design, immune regimen, and timing will last longest for each patient, and that forecast demand so organs are made before anyone needs them. The question shifts from who deserves an organ to which organ will work best in which patient, and how soon it can be there.

Payers and health systems. Model what happens to costs when a durable organ becomes something you can buy, and do it before the first invoice arrives.

The leap

Most predictions ask you to believe something that has not happened yet. This one rests on something that already has. A pig organ has kept a person alive. What remains is making it last.

You do not have to believe a cell can be made young, or anything about aging at all. You need only believe that a part which works for months can be made to work for years. That is still hard. It means finding modifications nobody has found yet, and proving they hold for a decade.

A hard leap, but not an impossible one.

The world still treats organ failure as permanent. It is not. Medicine is organized around scarcity. Once replacement becomes easier than repair, all of that reorganizes around abundance.

Replace parts as they wear out, and organ failure stops being a way you die.
